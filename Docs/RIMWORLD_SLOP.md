# My Thoughts on AI Slop Mods on the Workshop

Creating a mod really opened my eyes to how much work happens with stuff like Vanilla Expanded and the Alpha* mods and the amount of play testing and bug reports that support those creators.

## What about the moral and ethical reasons not to use AI?

My view is at this point AI has enough momentum that it is inevitable and it will be at least as transformative as the internet and smart phones. I don't have a crystal ball other than to tell you that in 2036 you will own devices that used LLMs as some part of their development and you **won't think twice about it**.

Personally I want it to stop being subsidized and cost what it actually costs so society has a chance of adapting to it.

In my adult life I experienced internet, the 2000s tech bubble, smart phones, 2008 housing collapse, covid pandemic and now large language models.

If you want to use sci-fi to understand the present I recommend reading:

* Future Shock by Alvin Toffler
* The Shockwave Rider by John Brunner
* Accelerando by Charles Stross

## My AI opinion

I'm in a field where [acquiring AI literacy is a must to keep my kids fed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JgVBqcqUGE0). There's a lot of reasons to be against AI, but boycotting isn't an option for me. It's a tool that I need to understand and know when to use to keep the lights on.

## Why we get slop mods

The side effects of AI tools:

* It's faster to create things with lower effort.
* It lowers the barrier of entry to people can create things beyond their level of skill or comprehension.

These two side effects allow people to create an overwhelming amount of slop. Not the fact that AI tools were used, but people saying "good enough" and shipping something that shouldn't have seen the light of day. I think specifically in the space of video game modding the problem is that people can use AI to create the mod but all the testing has to be done manually. Vibe coding without red-green TDD seems **insane** to me.

But going back to AI tools and modding, it creates a signal-to-noise problem because before the effort involved in creating a mod was a barrier that might have ensured a minimum level of quality.

## Checklist for having a good time with mods in the age of AI

* [ ] **Pay attention to the stars** Follow the modders who do good work so you alway see there new content.
* [ ] **Mod lists** Use RimSort's save/load mod list features so you don't brick your saves. Use something like VS Code and git to version control your mod lists so you can discard changes and get back to a working point.
* [ ] **Auto-saves** Keep a lot of autosaves so you can go back farther. Use [RimSaves](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=1713367505)
* [ ] **Maintainability** Does it have a github and an open source license? If it does that means it can be forked and taken over by the community.
* [ ] **Let it age** Don't install new mods when they are released because there will be bugs. Keep a [workshop collection to bookmark mods to try out](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=3569495245).
  * Don't look at how many mods you've bookmarked. Never look.
  * When you get to them six months later you'll be able to see if the mod was maintained or not, how many subscribes it has, how many collections it is in. All the indicators of quality to can't see the day it is first released.
